High-redshift quasars and their small- and large-scale environments

In 2023 and 2024, I led the analysis of a Cycle 1 JWST program to detect the host galaxies of five quasars in the Epoch of Reionization, above redshift 5. After performing careful image decomposition to disentangle the faint emission of the host galaxy from the bright quasar, we found that all five galaxies were significantly undermassive relative to their central supermassive black holes. Since the publication of the sample paper, a growing consensus has emerged that high-redshift quasars tend to lie above the local relationship between supermassive black hole mass and host galaxy stellar mass.

This phenomenon is at least partially driven by selection effects: because very massive galaxies are rare, the most massive black holes (the ones that can produce bright quasars) are actually found in lower-mass, more common galaxies (the Lauer bias). Even so, the fact remains that such extreme black hole-stellar mass ratios don't really exist in the nearby Universe, and there's usually an explanation when they do: uncertain dynamical modeling of the black hole mass, or stellar mass stripping by a massive neighbor.

Driven by this conundrum—extreme black hole mass-stellar mass ratios in the high-redshift Universe with no obvious analogues nearby—we obtained deeper JWST images of the field around one of these outliers: ULAS J1120+0641, at z=7.08. J1120+0641 has a billion-solar-mass black hole and a host galaxy that's only a few times more massive. Its gas reservoir is pretty pedestrian, so we wondered if this system was living in a hidden galaxy overdensity and if its host galaxy could potentially reach the local relation by accreting its neighbors.

PAH conditions near cosmic noon with efficient, spatially resolved spectra

I'm currently working with a sample of CO-detected main sequence galaxies near cosmic noon in the GOODS-N and COSMOS fields. We've obtained JWST/MIRI low-resolution spectroscopy, which at these redshifts covers the 3.3 and 6.2 micron PAH features, to explore the PAH-CO connection and PAH line ratios in this sample.

We obtained multiple pointings of the LRS slit on each object, which allowed us to create a custom data reduction and data cube building pipeline and study the spatially resolved emission in these galaxies. While the spatial and spectral resolution of our cubes are lower than the MRS, the observations are very efficient (11.5 hours of science time for a sample of 22 galaxies). I adapted the astrometric correction code developed for the SMILES survey and deep JADES MIRI parallel to work on spectroscopic JWST data using target acquisition and verification images. Recently, my code was used to correct LRS observations of the z=14.2 galaxy JADES-GS-z14-0.

Pre-JWST, only the most extreme starburst galaxies could have their PAH emission—particularly the 3.3 micron feature, tracing the smallest grains—characterized with Spitzer to understand their interstellar conditions. With JWST, we can start to build statistical samples of normal high-redshift PAH emission, which may help us understand the differences between galaxies at cosmic noon and galaxies today, and uncover the drivers of the significant decline in the cosmic star formation rate density in that time.

Mid-infrared galaxy number counts in the SMILES and JADES surveys

Find the paper here!

In 2024, I published new mid-infrared galaxy number counts in the SMILES survey (34 square arcmin, all MIRI bands from 5.6 to 25.5 microns at medium depth) and in the deep MIRI parallel of the JADES survey (9 square arcmin, 7.7 microns only). By leveraging these two surveys and archival data from previous generations of space telescopes, I was able to characterize the flux distribution of mid-infrared sources across 4-7 orders of magnitude in flux.

These counts have many uses! In the paper, I discuss the constraints they provide on the cosmic infrared background, but others have used them to infer cosmic star formation and black hole accretion histories, calibrate semi-analytical models, and even check the chances that mid-IR-detected substellar companions are actually background galaxies.

Stacked low-redshift (U)LIRG spectra as a pathway for PRIMA

Find the paper here!

As an undergraduate at the University of Massachusetts Amherst, I worked with Prof. Alexandra Pope to create stacks of Spitzer/IRS spectra from the GOALS sample of local, infrared-luminous galaxies. These high-SNR stacks, in bins of ascending AGN strength, reach longer wavelengths than JWST/MRS spectra and are publicly available for general use on VizieR here.

With these stacks, I derived a novel method to decompose the [O IV] 25.9 micron emission line into components from star formation and AGN activity, allowing [O IV] to be used as a robust AGN tracer down to low AGN luminosities. Others have used the stacks in forecasts of the PRIMA and SALTUS far-infrared probe mission concepts.
